Io is the innermost of the four Galilean moons of Jupiter and the most volcanically active world in the Solar System. Hundreds of volcanoes cover its surface, which is constantly renewed by lava flows and fallout from eruptions. Io is slightly larger than Earth's Moon.

## Quick facts

- Diameter: 3,643 km, slightly larger than the Moon (3,474 km)
- Average distance from Jupiter: about 421,700 km
- Orbital period: 1.77 Earth days
- Rotation: locked to its orbit, so the same face always points to Jupiter
- Surface: sulphur and sulphur dioxide frost, silicate lava
- Atmosphere: very thin, mostly sulphur dioxide
- Discovered: January 1610, by Galileo Galilei
- Volcanoes: more than 400 active volcanoes

## Volcanism

Io has more than 400 active volcanoes, more than any other body known. Some eruptions throw plumes of gas and dust hundreds of kilometres above the surface, and lava lakes and long lava flows cover large areas. One of the largest volcanic features is Loki Patera, a depression about 200 km across filled with a lake of molten lava whose surface periodically cools and cracks. The mountains of Io are not volcanoes: they are blocks of crust pushed up by the huge stresses in its rocks, and some, such as Boösaule Montes, rise to about 17 km.

## Why Io is so hot

The source of Io's heat is tidal heating, not radioactive decay. Jupiter's gravity pulls strongly on Io. Because Io is in an orbital resonance with Europa and Ganymede, whose pull keeps its orbit slightly oval, the distance between Io and Jupiter changes a little as it goes around. This makes the whole moon flex up and down by up to 100 metres, which generates enormous friction and heat inside it. The three moons Io, Europa and Ganymede complete orbits in a 1:2:4 ratio, which is known as the Laplace resonance.

## Surface and atmosphere

The surface of Io is coloured yellow, orange, red, white and black by sulphur compounds. It has almost no impact craters, because new lava and deposits constantly cover them: Io has the youngest surface of any solid body in the Solar System that has been studied. The thin atmosphere is mostly sulphur dioxide, supplied by volcanoes and by frost that evaporates when the sunlight hits it. Material from Io's volcanoes escapes into space and forms a doughnut-shaped ring of charged particles around Jupiter's orbit, called the Io plasma torus. It feeds Jupiter's magnetosphere.

## Exploration

- Voyager 1 passed Io in March 1979. The navigation engineer Linda Morabito spotted a volcanic plume on one of the images, the first time that active volcanoes were seen on another world.
- Galileo orbited Jupiter from 1995 to 2003 and flew by Io several times, observing eruptions in detail.
- Juno made two very close flybys of Io, in December 2023 and February 2024, at a distance of about 1,500 km.
