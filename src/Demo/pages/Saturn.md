Saturn is the sixth planet from the Sun and the second largest in the Solar System, after Jupiter. It is a gas giant famous for its spectacular system of bright rings, which is the largest and most massive ring system of any planet. Saturn is the least dense planet: its average density is lower than that of water.

## Quick facts

- Equatorial diameter: 120,536 km, about 9.5 times the diameter of Earth
- Mass: about 95 times the mass of Earth
- Average distance from the Sun: 1.43 billion km (9.58 astronomical units)
- Orbital period (year): 29.4 Earth years
- Rotation period: about 10 hours 40 minutes
- Average density: 0.69 g/cm³, less than water (1.0 g/cm³)
- Gravity at the 1-bar level: 9.0 m/s²
- Mean temperature at the 1-bar level: about −140 °C
- Moons: 274 confirmed (as of 2025)
- Rings: seven main rings, labelled with letters

## Atmosphere and interior

Saturn is made mostly of hydrogen (about 96%) and helium (about 3%). Its pale yellow and gold bands are formed by clouds of ammonia crystals and other compounds in the upper atmosphere. Winds near the equator blow at up to about 1,800 km/h. Deep inside, the pressure is so high that hydrogen turns into a liquid metal, around a core of rock and ice whose exact size is uncertain. Like Jupiter, Saturn radiates more heat than it absorbs from the Sun.

A strange, six-sided jet stream called the hexagon surrounds the north pole. It is about 29,000 km wide, wider than Earth, and was first seen by the Voyager spacecraft. A giant storm sits at the south pole, and every few decades a huge white storm, called a Great White Spot, erupts in the northern hemisphere.

## Rings

Saturn's rings are made of countless particles of ice, ranging in size from tiny grains to boulders as big as a house, with a little rock and dust. Their composition is about 99.9% water ice. The main rings extend up to about 282,000 km from the planet but are extremely thin, typically only about 10 metres thick.

- The three main rings are called A, B and C. The B ring is the brightest and densest.
- The Cassini Division, a gap about 4,800 km wide, separates the A and B rings.
- Faint outer rings, such as the E ring, are fed by material from Enceladus.

Galileo saw the rings in 1610 but could not identify them. Christiaan Huygens recognised that they were a ring around the planet in 1655. How old the rings are is still debated. Because Saturn's axis is tilted by 26.7 degrees, the rings appear more or less open as the planet orbits, and about every 15 years they are seen edge-on and almost seem to vanish.

## Moons

Saturn has 274 confirmed moons, which is the largest number of any planet. In March 2025 the International Astronomical Union recognised 128 newly discovered moons, which raised the total from 146. Most of the new moons are small irregular moons, only a few kilometres across, on distant orbits. The most important are:

- Titan, the largest, with a thick atmosphere
- Enceladus, which sprays water from its south pole
- Mimas, which has a giant crater that makes it look like the Death Star
- Rhea, Iapetus, Dione and Tethys, medium-sized icy moons

## Exploration

- Pioneer 11 made the first flyby in 1979. Voyager 1 followed in November 1980 and Voyager 2 in August 1981.
- Cassini–Huygens, a joint mission of NASA, ESA and the Italian space agency, was launched on 15 October 1997 and entered orbit around Saturn on 1 July 2004. The Huygens probe landed on Titan on 14 January 2005.
- Cassini studied Saturn, its rings and its moons for 13 years. The mission ended on 15 September 2017, when the spacecraft was deliberately sent into Saturn's atmosphere to avoid contaminating moons that might be habitable.
