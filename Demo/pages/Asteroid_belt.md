The asteroid belt is a doughnut-shaped region between the orbits of Mars and Jupiter that contains millions of rocky and metallic bodies called asteroids. It holds the debris of the early Solar System, which never came together to form a planet. Contrary to its depiction in films, the belt is mostly empty space.

## Quick facts

- Location: between the orbits of Mars and Jupiter
- Distance from the Sun: roughly 2.2 to 3.2 astronomical units (about 330 to 480 million km)
- Number of asteroids: millions, with well over a million larger than 1 km across
- Total mass: about 3 to 4% of the mass of the Moon
- Largest object: Ceres, about 940 km across
- Other large asteroids: Vesta, Pallas and Hygiea

## Origin

Planets formed from a disk of gas and dust around the young Sun. In the region between Mars and Jupiter, the gravity of the giant planet Jupiter stirred up the orbits of the small bodies called planetesimals. They collided too violently and too often to stick together, so no planet formed. Most of the original material was thrown out of the region, and what remains today is only a tiny fraction of the original mass.

## Structure and composition

Together, the four largest objects, Ceres, Vesta, Pallas and Hygiea, hold more than half of the mass of the belt. Ceres alone holds roughly one third of it (estimates range from about a quarter to about 40%). Asteroids differ widely in composition:

- C-type asteroids are dark and rich in carbon. They are the most common, about three quarters of the total, and are found mostly in the outer belt.
- S-type asteroids are bright and made of silicate rock and metal. They are found mostly in the inner belt.
- M-type asteroids are made largely of metal, mainly iron and nickel.

The density of the belt varies. Gaps called Kirkwood gaps appear at distances where an orbital period would be a simple fraction of Jupiter's, such as one third, because Jupiter's gravity pushes the asteroids out of those orbits.

## Is the belt dangerous to spacecraft?

No. The asteroids are spread over a huge volume, and the average distance between two large asteroids is usually hundreds of thousands of kilometres. Many spacecraft have crossed the belt without needing to dodge anything, and mission planners have to choose a target in advance and aim for it.

## Exploration

- Galileo made the first close flybys of asteroids, Gaspra in 1991 and Ida in 1993, where it found the tiny moon Dactyl.
- NEAR Shoemaker orbited the near-Earth asteroid Eros and landed on it in February 2001.
- Dawn orbited Vesta from 2011 to 2012 and Ceres from 2015 to 2018.
- Lucy, launched by NASA in October 2021, visited the main-belt asteroid Donaldjohanson in April 2025 on its way to the Trojan asteroids of Jupiter.
- Psyche, launched by NASA in October 2023, is travelling to the metal-rich asteroid 16 Psyche, due to arrive in 2029.

Some missions have visited asteroids that come from the belt but now orbit near Earth. Hayabusa2, from Japan, returned a sample of the asteroid Ryugu in December 2020, and NASA's OSIRIS-REx brought back a sample of the asteroid Bennu in September 2023. On 26 September 2022 NASA's DART spacecraft hit the small asteroid Dimorphos, and changed its orbit by about 32 minutes, the first demonstration of a technique to deflect an asteroid.

## Meteorites

Most meteorites that fall on Earth come from the asteroid belt. Asteroids collide with each other or have their orbits changed by Jupiter's gravity, and fragments can enter orbits that cross Earth's. Studying meteorites shows how old the Solar System is, about 4.57 billion years.
