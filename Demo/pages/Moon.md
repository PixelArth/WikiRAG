The Moon is Earth's only natural satellite and the fifth largest moon in the Solar System. It is the brightest object in the night sky and the only world beyond Earth that humans have visited. The Moon always shows the same face to Earth, and its gravity is the main cause of ocean tides.

## Quick facts

- Diameter: 3,474 km, about 27% of Earth's diameter
- Average distance from Earth: 384,400 km
- Orbital period around Earth: 27.3 days
- Time between two full moons: 29.5 days
- Rotation: once per orbit, so the same side always faces Earth
- Surface gravity: 1.6 m/s², about one-sixth of Earth's
- Mass: about 1.2% of Earth's mass (1/81)
- Mean density: 3.34 g/cm³
- Surface temperature: roughly −133 °C at night and up to about 120 °C by day near the equator
- Atmosphere: practically none

## Origin

The most widely accepted explanation is the giant impact hypothesis. About 4.5 billion years ago, soon after Earth formed, a body about the size of Mars, called Theia, struck the young Earth. The debris thrown into orbit gathered together to form the Moon. This explains why the Moon is made of material similar to Earth's mantle and has only a small iron core.

## Surface features

The Moon's surface is covered by a layer of broken rock and dust called regolith.

- The highlands are bright, old and heavily cratered.
- The maria (singular: mare, Latin for "sea") are dark, smooth plains of solidified basaltic lava that filled large impact basins billions of years ago. They are not real seas.
- The South Pole–Aitken basin, on the far side, is about 2,500 km wide, one of the largest known impact craters in the Solar System.
- Famous craters include Tycho and Copernicus, surrounded by bright rays of ejected material.

The Moon has a near side and a far side. The far side is not "dark": it receives as much sunlight as the near side, but it can never be seen from Earth. It has far fewer maria.

## Phases, tides and eclipses

As the Moon orbits Earth, we see different portions of its sunlit half. This produces the phases, a cycle of new moon, crescent, first quarter, gibbous, full moon and back again every 29.5 days.

The gravity of the Moon pulls on Earth's oceans and creates two tidal bulges, which produces roughly two high tides and two low tides a day. The Moon is also slowly moving away from Earth, by about 3.8 cm each year.

A solar eclipse happens when the Moon passes between the Sun and Earth and blocks the Sun. A lunar eclipse happens when Earth passes between the Sun and the Moon and its shadow falls on the Moon.

## Exploration

- Luna 2, from the Soviet Union, was the first spacecraft to reach the Moon, in 1959. Luna 9 made the first soft landing in 1966.
- Apollo 11 landed on 20 July 1969. Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin walked on the surface while Michael Collins orbited above, and they became the first humans on the Moon.
- Six crewed missions landed on the Moon between 1969 and 1972, and twelve astronauts in total walked on its surface. Apollo 17, in December 1972, was the last. The missions brought back about 382 kg of rock and soil.
- In 2019 China's Chang'e 4 made the first soft landing on the far side. In August 2023 India's Chandrayaan-3 landed near the Moon's south polar region.
- NASA's Artemis programme was designed to return astronauts to the Moon and to build a long-term presence there.

## Water and future interest

Spacecraft have found water ice inside permanently shadowed craters near the lunar poles, where the temperature stays extremely low. This ice could provide drinking water and rocket fuel for future missions, which is one reason the polar regions are the targets of many planned landings.
