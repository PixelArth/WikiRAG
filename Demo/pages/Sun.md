The Sun is the star at the centre of the Solar System. It is a nearly perfect ball of hot plasma, classified as a G-type main-sequence star (spectral type G2V) and often called a yellow dwarf. The Sun holds about 99.86% of the total mass of the Solar System, and its gravity keeps every planet, asteroid and comet in orbit around it. Sunlight takes about 8 minutes and 20 seconds to reach Earth.

## Quick facts

- Type: G2V main-sequence star (yellow dwarf)
- Diameter: about 1.39 million km, roughly 109 times the diameter of Earth
- Mass: about 1.99 × 10^30 kg, roughly 333,000 times the mass of Earth
- Average distance from Earth: about 149.6 million km (1 astronomical unit)
- Age: about 4.6 billion years
- Surface temperature: about 5,500 °C (effective temperature 5,772 K)
- Core temperature: about 15 million °C
- Rotation: about 25 days at the equator and about 35 days near the poles
- Planets in orbit: 8, plus dwarf planets, asteroids and comets

## Structure and composition

The Sun is made of about 73% hydrogen and 25% helium by mass. Heavier elements such as oxygen, carbon, neon and iron make up the remaining 2% or so. Because the Sun is a ball of gas, it has no solid surface, and it is built in layers.

### Interior

- The core extends to about a quarter of the Sun's radius. This is where fusion happens, at about 15 million °C.
- The radiative zone carries energy outward as light. A photon can take tens of thousands of years, or more, to cross it.
- The convective zone, the outer third of the radius, carries energy upward in rising and sinking currents of plasma.

### Atmosphere

- The photosphere is the visible "surface", about 500 km thick, at roughly 5,500 °C.
- The chromosphere is a thin reddish layer above it, visible during total solar eclipses.
- The corona is the outermost atmosphere. It is millions of degrees hot, far hotter than the photosphere below it, and the reason for this is still an active research question called the coronal heating problem.

## Energy production

The Sun shines because of nuclear fusion in its core. Under enormous pressure and temperature, hydrogen nuclei fuse into helium, mainly through a process called the proton–proton chain. Every second, the Sun fuses roughly 600 million tonnes of hydrogen into helium, and about 4 million tonnes of matter are converted into energy as a result. That energy escapes as sunlight and as a stream of particles. Only a tiny fraction of the Sun's output reaches Earth, yet it drives the weather, the oceans and almost all life.

## Solar cycle and space weather

The Sun's magnetic activity rises and falls in a cycle of about 11 years. At the peak of the cycle, many sunspots appear. A sunspot is a region cooler than its surroundings, roughly 3,500 to 4,000 °C, which looks dark only by contrast.

- A solar flare is a sudden burst of radiation from the Sun's atmosphere.
- A coronal mass ejection is a huge cloud of magnetised plasma thrown into space.
- The solar wind is a constant flow of charged particles that streams past all the planets.

When these particles reach Earth, they can cause auroras and, in strong storms, disturb satellites, radio communication and power grids.

## Life cycle

The Sun is about halfway through its life as a main-sequence star, which lasts about 10 billion years in total. It is very slowly getting brighter. In roughly 5 billion years the hydrogen in the core will run out. The Sun will then swell into a red giant, shed its outer layers, and finally shrink into a white dwarf, a dense and slowly cooling remnant about the size of Earth.

## Observation and exploration

Looking directly at the Sun can permanently damage the eyes, and observers use special filters. Spacecraft study the Sun from many viewpoints:

- SOHO, a joint ESA and NASA mission, has watched the Sun from the Earth–Sun Lagrange point L1 since 1995.
- The Solar Dynamics Observatory, launched by NASA in 2010, images the Sun continuously in many wavelengths.
- Parker Solar Probe, launched by NASA in 2018, flies through the corona. On 24 December 2024 it passed about 6.1 million km from the Sun's surface at a speed of about 692,000 km/h, the closest approach and the fastest speed ever reached by a human-made object.
- Solar Orbiter, an ESA and NASA mission launched in 2020, takes the first images of the Sun's poles.
