Enceladus is a small, icy moon of Saturn, only about 500 km across. Despite its size, it is one of the most scientifically exciting objects in the Solar System: jets of water vapour and ice grains erupt from cracks at its south pole, and they come from a global ocean of liquid water beneath the ice shell.

## Quick facts

- Diameter: about 504 km
- Average distance from Saturn: about 238,000 km
- Orbital period: 1.37 Earth days
- Rotation: locked to its orbit, so the same face always points to Saturn
- Surface: fresh water ice, one of the most reflective surfaces in the Solar System
- Discovered: 28 August 1789, by William Herschel
- Ocean: global, salty liquid water under an ice shell

## Surface

The surface of Enceladus is covered with fresh, clean ice that reflects nearly all of the sunlight reaching it, which makes the moon extremely bright and also very cold, about −200 °C. The terrain is varied. Old, cratered regions are found mostly near the equator, while the south pole has a young, smooth surface with almost no craters. The region around the south pole is crossed by four long parallel fractures nicknamed the tiger stripes, because they look like claw marks on the ice.

## Plumes

In 2005 the Cassini spacecraft discovered jets of water vapour, ice grains and other compounds spraying from the tiger stripes at the south pole into space. These plumes reach hundreds of kilometres in height. Some of the particles fall back as snow, and the rest escape and supply Saturn's E ring, the faint outer ring that follows the orbit of Enceladus. On 28 October 2015, Cassini flew through the plumes at a distance of only 49 km from the surface to sample them directly.

## Subsurface ocean

Measurements of the gravity and of the wobble of Enceladus show that a global ocean of liquid water lies beneath an ice shell that is about 20 to 25 km thick on average, and much thinner, perhaps 5 km, at the south pole. Heat for the ocean comes from tidal flexing caused by Saturn's gravity and by an orbital resonance with the moon Dione.

## Ingredients for life

Cassini's instruments analysed the plume material and found, among other things:

- salts, so that the water is salty
- organic molecules
- molecular hydrogen, found in 2017, which can be a source of chemical energy
- tiny grains of silica, found in 2015, which point to hot water reacting with rock on the seafloor
- phosphorus, in the form of phosphates, reported in 2023 in a study of old Cassini data

These results show that the ocean contains many of the ingredients needed for life as we know it. They do not show that there is life.

## Exploration

Voyager 1 and 2 imaged Enceladus in 1980 and 1981 and noticed its bright, young surface. Cassini studied it in detail from 2004 to 2017. No dedicated mission to Enceladus has been approved so far, but several concepts of an orbiter and a lander, designed to sample the plumes and search for signs of life, have been proposed.
